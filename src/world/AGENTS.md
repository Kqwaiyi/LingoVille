# world

The React Three Fiber scene: town, character controller (Rapier), NPCs, day–night, interaction triggers, sign textures. Three.js is pinned at r186; don't bump it.

**Rules**
- Read game state only through store selectors; change it only through store actions.
- Positions live in the Rapier world. Only the current place id goes into sim state.
- `sim`, `content` and `ai` may not import this module.
- The town is built from kit pieces in `public/town/town.glb`, built by `npm run build:town` (`scripts/build-town.ts`) from the town's own Blender pieces (`art/town/`: the buildings, street lamps, tram track and stops, trees and most of the furniture) and Kenney's CC0 kits (the rest): rebuild it, don't edit the GLB. `Piece` (`Kit.tsx`) draws one; `Instances` draws many as instanced meshes, which is how the scenery, furniture and upper floors are drawn (keep draw calls down: use it for anything repeated or static). The names the game asks of the art live in `townArt.ts`. Façades, roofs and layout are the same in every Culture Pack; only `Props`, `Signs` and the groceries change with the pack.
- `town.ts` lays out the places: the buildings, the street, the tram stops and the park, and everything in them. What's inside a building is placed in the building's own frame (`inside(placeId)`: x along the front wall, z out of the front door), and what's in the park from its centre, so a building can move or turn and keep its insides. `scenery.ts` lays out what only dresses the town (the buildings nobody goes into, street furniture, the canal, the backdrop); nothing in it is a place or matters to the game.
- Anything that stands taller than a storey (upper floors, roofs, the buildings nobody goes into) lifts off while it stands between the camera and the Character, so they're never hidden. Walls stay.
- Moving a building or a tram stop can change which stop is nearest a place: update `STOP_PLACES` in content to match. `tooling/stopPlaces.test.ts` checks it, and `tooling/townLayout.test.ts` checks that everyone and everything is in its place with no furniture where anyone stands.
- Per-pack props are pieces of `town.glb` too, one per `PROP_IDS` (built from `scripts/town-props.ts`). A pack lists the props it sets out at each place; `setDressing.ts` keeps the shared spots for them, by kind (door, counter, table, floor, lawn, platform, shelf), and `setOut` puts each prop in the next free spot of its kind. `tooling/setDressing.test.ts` fails a pack whose props don't fit. Signs are painted from pack strings: every place has a name board, places with a door have their hours beside it, and the café and restaurant have menus.
- Every colour the world draws is a name in `palette.ts`: `PALETTE`, the one shared palette, the sky's own `SKY_PALETTE`, or Clawd's orange (`CLAWD_PALETTE`). No `#hex` colour literals anywhere else in this folder: `tooling/townArt.test.ts` fails them, and fails town art off the palette; `tooling/characterArt.test.ts` fails character art off it.
- Every material in the town art is a palette colour and a surface (`surfaces.ts`: plaster, brick, stone, tile, wood, planks, fabric, foliage, grass, cobbles, paving and so on), named `colour:surface` (a bare colour is matte). A repaint swaps the colour and keeps the surface, so a building's plaster stays plaster in its own colour. `materials.ts` draws them physically based and smooth shaded (nothing is flat shaded any more): each surface's roughness and shine, its detail texture (`public/town/surfaces/`, built by `npm run build:surfaces`) projected from the world on three sides so it needs no texture coordinates and keeps its size on a stretched piece, the colour chalked a little by the weather (`fade`), mottled across metres so repeats don't show, and grimier at the foot of a wall. Plain shapes (kerbs, paving, the ground) take `townMaterial(colour, surface)`; don't make materials of your own for the town. `tooling/townArt.test.ts` fails a material that isn't a palette colour and a known surface, and a textured surface without its texture.
- Glass and water are the exceptions. Window glass by day is dark and mirrors the sky, darker still while its place is closed; lit at night, a wall's window shows a room behind it (interior mapping in `litGlass`), with its own walls, floor, picture or curtains, and other glass glows lamplight. The pond and the canal are `WATER`, rippling and mirroring the sky.
- Day and night: `daylight` in `sim` says from the game clock which two lighting presets to blend, where the key light, the sun and the moon stand and whether the lamps are on (its times live in `DAYLIGHT` in `tuning.ts`). `Lighting.tsx` holds what each preset looks like (sky, clouds, haze, sun, moon, stars and lights, in palette colours; the sky's own are `SKY_PALETTE`, kept out of what the art builds snap to), adds the glow of sunrise and sunset while the sun is near the horizon, and blends them each frame; `Sky.tsx` draws the sky and its drifting clouds. The town is lit by the sky itself as well as the sun: every few frames the sky (without its sun and moon) is drawn into a small cube and prefiltered as the scene's environment, which lights rough surfaces from all round and is what glass and water mirror. Start-up is kept short: the game opens first (the title screen reads the saves, or a dev game starts) and the town comes into view after, a few materials a frame, each compiled before it's shown (`Precompiled` in `Scene.tsx`), so no one frame compiles every shader at once and holds the page up; and the sky's light, whose prefiltering shader is slow to compile, comes up a couple of seconds after the town appears, against a dark stand-in the materials were compiled for. Mind the cost of a new shader or light: the smoke tests give the town 5 seconds to load, two at a time. From dusk, the lamps nearest the Character give real light (a small pool of point lights, moved to the nearest of `LAMP_SPOTS` in `lamps.ts`); the rest only glow. The haze is light and only reaches the far hills and mountains and the land beyond the ground, which runs on to the horizon so the ground's edge never shows. The key light's shadows cover the ground ahead of the camera and fade out at the shadow map's edges, a patch to three's shadow shader that fails loudly if three changes it. A window is clear while its place is open and dark while it's closed; from dusk, open places' windows and the street lights are lit, sharing one lamplight material that glows brighter as night falls.
- `Effects.tsx` is what the camera does with the light: ambient occlusion (N8AO), bloom, filmic tone mapping (ACES), a light grade (a touch of contrast and colour, and a soft vignette) and SMAA, from `@react-three/postprocessing`, which turns the renderer's own tone mapping off. Bloom takes only what's brighter than its threshold, and only lamplight at night is lit that bright (bulbs, lit windows' rooms): keep sunlit walls, the sky and everything else under it. `postprocessing` is pinned to a version whose three peer range takes r186; check that range before bumping it. The game can't choose the graphics card (on a laptop with two, the browser's or Windows' setting does), so it suits itself to the one it gets: it starts with the full look and, while the frames come slow, puts the next cheaper level on trial (`graphicsLevel.ts`: ambient occlusion goes first, then the pixel ratio), keeping it only if the frames come faster for it. A computer held up by its processor rather than its graphics card keeps the full look.
- Everyone (the Character, Named NPCs, Shift Customers and Passers-by) is Clawd, the Claude Code mascot, drawn by `CharacterFigure` from an Appearance Preset: what they wear (a hat, eyewear and neckwear, tinted in the look's colour) is what tells them apart, and staff wear an apron in their role's colour. Nobody has a human body, face, hair or skin. The art, `public/characters/clawd.glb` (the figure on its rig, everything anyone wears, the eyes' shape keys and the clips), is modelled, rigged and animated in Blender by `art/characters/clawd.py` (see its `README.md`): rebuild it, don't edit the GLB. The names the game asks of the art live in `characterArt.ts`, and `tooling/characterArt.test.ts` fails art that lacks one. An NPC's Patience shows only as `CharacterFigure`'s `expression` (the eyes' shape, the body's tilt or nod, a drop of sweat), in town and in the conversation column's portrait (`NpcPortrait`); the eyes blink now and then on their own. The shops' counters stand at `COUNTER_HEIGHT` (`town.ts`), low enough for Clawd to see over, so the staff behind one show their faces.

- To look at the town from the same spot each time, the dev-only `?camera=x,y,z,tx,ty,tz` holds the camera at (x, y, z) looking at (tx, ty, tz) instead of following the Character (with `?newGame&at=<hour>&spawn=<place>`, any time of day from anywhere). Something standing between that camera and the Character still lifts off.

**Testing**: no automated 3D visual tests and no scene-graph tests. Put rules in `sim` and test them there. A pure helper with no Three in it (`graphicsLevel.ts`, `lamps.ts`) is tested beside it with Vitest. Cover player-visible behaviour in the Playwright smoke.
